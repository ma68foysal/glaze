# Demo store blog posts

Three articles for the demo store's **News** blog. Written to match the theme's
own copy voice — ingredient-literate, plain, anti-hype — so the blog reads as
part of the same brand as the homepage.

The Theme Store requires authentic text on every demo store page, and your
`blog.json` and `article.json` templates will both be reviewed. Three posts is
enough for the index grid to look composed rather than half-populated.

**Where to add:** Shopify admin → Content → Blog posts → Add blog post → set the
blog to **News**.

**Each post needs:** title, content, excerpt, featured image, author, tags.
The blog index renders title, excerpt, image and author; the article page adds
tags, share links and comments.

**Featured images:** use skincare photography you hold rights to, or
[Shopify Burst](https://burst.shopify.com). No text baked into the image —
that's a demo store requirement.

## Post 1

**Title:** How long a new product actually takes to work

**Excerpt:** Four weeks is the honest answer for most actives, and the first two
can look like nothing is happening. Here's what to expect week by week.

**Tags:** `routine`, `actives`, `getting started`

**Author:** Suggest a real name on your team

**Content:**

```html
<p>The most common message we get is some version of the same question: it's been ten days, why can't I see anything yet?</p>

<p>The honest answer is that most of what a good active does in the first fortnight happens below the surface, where you can't see it. Skin turns over roughly every 28 days. Until a full cycle has completed, you're looking at skin that was already on its way to the surface before you started.</p>

<h2>Week one to two</h2>

<p>Hydration changes first. Humectants work immediately, so skin feels softer and looks less tight within a few days. That's real, but it isn't the active doing its job yet — it's water.</p>

<p>If you've started a retinoid or an acid, this is also when irritation shows up if it's going to. Mild tightness or flaking is normal. Stinging, persistent redness or burning is not, and means you should drop back to every third night.</p>

<h2>Week three to four</h2>

<p>Texture starts to shift. Congestion clears more easily, and skin holds makeup differently. This is the point most people notice something has changed without being able to name what.</p>

<h2>Week six to twelve</h2>

<p>Tone and pigmentation. These are the slowest changes because they depend on cell turnover happening repeatedly, not once. Clinical studies on vitamin C and niacinamide typically run twelve weeks for exactly this reason.</p>

<h2>What actually goes wrong</h2>

<p>Almost always, it's switching too early. Six weeks in, nothing dramatic has happened, so a new serum goes in — and the clock resets. The products that work are the ones you use long enough to find out whether they work.</p>

<p>Pick a simple routine, photograph your skin in the same light on day one, and don't change anything for eight weeks. Then look at the photographs rather than the mirror.</p>
```

## Post 2

**Title:** Why we leave fragrance out

**Excerpt:** Fragrance is the most common cause of cosmetic contact dermatitis,
and it does nothing for your skin. Leaving it out was the easiest decision we
made.

**Tags:** `ingredients`, `sensitive skin`, `formulation`

**Author:** Same author as post 1, or a second name

**Content:**

```html
<p>Fragrance makes a product pleasant to use. It's also the single most common cause of cosmetic contact dermatitis, and it contributes nothing to how a formula performs.</p>

<p>That trade was easy for us to refuse.</p>

<h2>The part that's hard to check</h2>

<p>On an ingredient list, fragrance appears as one word — <em>parfum</em> or <em>fragrance</em> — regardless of how many individual compounds sit behind it. A single fragrance accord can contain dozens. Trade secret protections mean that's entirely legal, and it also means someone reacting to a product has almost no way to identify what they reacted to.</p>

<p>If you have sensitive or compromised skin, that's a genuine problem. You can't avoid an ingredient you can't see.</p>

<h2>"Unscented" isn't the same thing</h2>

<p>Unscented often means masking fragrance has been added to cover the smell of the base. Fragrance-free means none was added at all.</p>

<p>Our formulas smell like what's in them. Some are faintly herbal from the plant oils, some smell of very little. That's the raw material, not an addition.</p>

<h2>Essential oils are still fragrance</h2>

<p>Worth saying plainly, because "naturally derived" gets used as though it settles the question. Linalool and limonene are among the most frequently reported fragrance allergens, and both occur naturally in lavender and citrus oils. Botanical origin doesn't make a sensitiser less sensitising.</p>

<p>We don't use them for scent. Where a plant oil appears in one of our formulas, it's there for what it does to the skin barrier.</p>

<h2>What you give up</h2>

<p>Some of the ritual. A fragranced cleanser is a nicer three minutes than an unfragranced one, and we understand why people miss it.</p>

<p>What you get is a shorter ingredient list where every line has a job, and a product you can keep using on a week when your skin is already unhappy.</p>
```

## Post 3

**Title:** The order to layer a simple routine

**Excerpt:** Thinnest to thickest covers most of it. Here's the short version,
plus the two places that rule breaks.

**Tags:** `routine`, `how to`, `getting started`

**Author:** Either author

**Content:**

```html
<p>Layering advice gets complicated quickly, usually by people selling a tenth step. For a routine of four or five products, one rule covers almost everything: <strong>thinnest to thickest</strong>.</p>

<p>Water-based products go on before oil-based ones. A thin serum can't get through a moisturiser, but a moisturiser has no trouble going on over a serum.</p>

<h2>Morning</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Cleanser</strong> — or just water, if your skin is dry and you cleansed properly the night before.</li>
  <li><strong>Water-based serum</strong> — vitamin C, niacinamide, hydrating actives.</li>
  <li><strong>Moisturiser</strong> — seals the layers underneath.</li>
  <li><strong>Sunscreen</strong> — always last, always in the morning. Everything else in your routine works harder when this one is consistent.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Evening</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Cleanser</strong> — twice if you've worn sunscreen or makeup. Oil or balm first, then your usual cleanser.</li>
  <li><strong>Treatment</strong> — retinoid or acid, on the nights you use one.</li>
  <li><strong>Serum</strong> — hydrating, if you use one.</li>
  <li><strong>Moisturiser or facial oil</strong> — oil last, if you use both.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Where the rule breaks</h2>

<p><strong>Retinoids on damp skin.</strong> Applying a retinoid to skin that's still damp increases penetration, which sounds good and mostly means more irritation. Wait until skin is properly dry — a few minutes is enough.</p>

<p><strong>Buffering.</strong> If a retinoid is too much, moisturiser <em>before</em> it slows absorption and reduces irritation. It breaks the thin-to-thick rule on purpose, and it's a legitimate way to stay consistent while your skin adjusts.</p>

<h2>Waiting between layers</h2>

<p>You don't need to. The often-repeated advice about waiting for a specific pH to normalise between steps applies to a narrow set of formulas and almost never to a routine like this one. Let each layer absorb enough that the next one goes on evenly — usually under a minute.</p>

<p>A routine you follow every night beats a more sophisticated one you follow twice a week.</p>
```
